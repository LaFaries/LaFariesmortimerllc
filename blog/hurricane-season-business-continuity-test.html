Hurricane Season Is a Business Continuity Test

LaFaries Mortimer LLC · 3 min read · June 2026

Here in South Florida, June 1 means more than the start of summer. It is the start of hurricane season — and it runs all the way through November 30.

Most business owners prepare their homes. They buy water, check the generator, and put up shutters. But very few prepare their business operations with the same seriousness.

The real question

If your office were closed for two weeks, could your business — or your ministry — keep operating?

For many organizations, the honest answer is no. Not because they lack commitment, but because everything important lives in one building, on one computer, or in one person's head.

What a storm actually exposes

A hurricane does not create operational weaknesses. It reveals the ones that were already there.

Common gaps a storm reveals
  • Contracts, licenses, and tax records stored only in paper files
  • One office computer holding the only copy of client data
  • No way to invoice, accept payments, or answer email remotely
  • Staff and volunteers with no clear plan for who does what
  • Insurance policies no one can locate when it is time to file a claim

Your business continuity checklist

Business continuity simply means your organization can keep serving people when normal operations are disrupted. Before the next storm, walk through these:

  • Digitize critical documents. Articles of organization, EIN letter, licenses, leases, insurance policies, and contracts — scanned and stored in secure cloud storage.
  • Back up off-site. If your only backup is an external drive sitting next to the computer, it will flood with the computer.
  • Confirm remote access. Can you send invoices, take payments, and reach your email from a phone or laptop anywhere?
  • Build a contact tree. Who calls whom — staff, volunteers, key clients, vendors — and how will you reach them if phones are down?
  • Photograph your space and equipment. Before-storm photos make insurance claims far easier.
  • Write a one-page continuity plan. Who makes decisions, where operations move, and how you will communicate with the people you serve.
  • Plan for cash flow. Know how long you can operate if revenue pauses for a few weeks.

For churches and ministries

Ministries carry an extra responsibility. When a storm passes, the community often turns to the church first — for food, shelter, information, and comfort.

That means your ministry needs to be organized before the storm, so it can serve after it. Membership records, giving records, volunteer rosters, and online giving should all be reachable even if the building is closed. Service announcements should be able to go out by text, email, social media, and your website within minutes.

Preparation is a form of stewardship

I believe preparation is stewardship. The business, the ministry, and the people depending on them are worth protecting with structure — not just hope.
